# The Documented Costs and Harms of Twice-Yearly Clock Changes

*Research compiled July 2026, in support of a synthesis report on the House's July 14, 2026 passage of the Sunshine Protection Act (H.R. 139), which would make daylight saving time (DST) permanent nationwide.*

## Overview

A large and largely convergent body of epidemiological research finds that the biannual transitions between standard time (ST) and daylight saving time — not DST or ST as ongoing states, but the *acute act of shifting the clock* — produce measurable, short-lived spikes in fatal accidents, cardiovascular events, workplace injuries, and other harms. These effects are concentrated in the days immediately following the March "spring forward" transition, are attributed mainly to one hour of acute sleep loss and circadian misalignment, and mostly fade within a week. The fall "back" transition shows much weaker and more mixed effects on sleep-linked outcomes, but a separate and well-established literature finds that fall's shift of darkness into the evening commute raises pedestrian and cyclist deaths. It is important to keep two questions distinct, addressed in Section 5: the harm of *switching* clocks twice a year (which nearly everyone agrees should stop) versus the health merits of *permanent DST* versus *permanent standard time* as the state to land on — sleep scientists overwhelmingly prefer the latter, which the Sunshine Protection Act would not deliver.

## 1. Traffic Fatalities

**Fritz, Fischer, Zerr, McMahon & Ganzenmüller, "A Chronobiological Evaluation of the Acute Effects of Daylight Saving Time on Traffic Accident Risk," *Current Biology*, 2020** — the definitive large-sample study. Using 732,835 fatal motor vehicle accidents from the U.S. Fatality Analysis Reporting System (FARS), 1996–2017, the authors found fatal crash risk rose about **6%** in the workweek following the spring-forward transition, with the increase driven especially by morning crashes and by locations further west within a time zone (where the sun rises later relative to clock time). Using a rough population-scaled extrapolation, this is commonly reported as **roughly 28 additional traffic deaths per year**. A striking secondary finding: the 2007 Energy Policy Act extension of DST (moving the start from the first Sunday in April to the second Sunday in March) *doubled* the morning-crash effect — from about 5–6% pre-2007 to about **13%** post-2007 — because the March transition occurs when mornings are still darker, worsening the mismatch between clock time and dawn. Critically, the study found **no significant fall-back effect** on fatal crash risk, and no support for the claim that DST reduces evening crashes through better afternoon light — evidence the authors say points to sleep deprivation/circadian misalignment (not general lighting) as the driver of the spring effect.

A related follow-up analysis of FARS data 2010–2019 found a roughly **12% increase in fatal crashes involving vehicle occupants** in the weeks after the spring shift, consistent with Fritz et al.'s direction though a higher point estimate — a reminder that estimates vary somewhat by sample window and methodology, even though the qualitative finding (spring-only risk spike) is robust across studies.

**Fall-back and pedestrians:** while sleep-linked crash risk does not rise in autumn, a separate and well-replicated literature shows the *earlier onset of evening darkness* after the fall transition sharply raises pedestrian and cyclist fatalities. Because roughly 75–77% of pedestrian fatalities already occur in darkness, and clocks abruptly shift sunset an hour earlier into the high-traffic 5–7 p.m. commute window, pedestrian/bicyclist fatal crashes rise an estimated **13%** in the weeks following the fall transition; one widely cited estimate is that **roughly 171 pedestrian deaths per year** could be avoided if the clock were not set back in autumn (IIHS-linked and related NHTSA-data analyses). Other analyses put the pedestrian-fatality jump on the day after the fall change specifically as high as low single digits (~1–2%) with several hundred extra fatalities annually when aggregated across the vulnerable post-transition weeks. Net effect: spring transitions are dangerous mainly through sleep loss/circadian disruption; fall transitions are dangerous mainly through abrupt darkness during peak pedestrian travel — two different mechanisms, both traceable to the *act of switching* rather than to DST or ST as a steady state.

## 2. Cardiovascular Effects

**Janszky & Ljung, *New England Journal of Medicine* (2008)** is the foundational study: using the Swedish register of >100,000 myocardial infarctions, they found the incidence rate ratio (IRR) for acute MI (AMI) rose to about **1.05 (5%)** in the first week after the spring transition, while AMI incidence *fell* slightly in the days after the fall transition — an early demonstration of the same asymmetric, sleep-loss-driven pattern later confirmed for traffic deaths.

**Sandhu, Seth & Gurm, *Open Heart*, 2014** — the widely cited "Michigan" study — examined 42,060 hospital admissions for PCI-treated heart attacks in Michigan's BMC2 registry (2010–2013) and found AMI admissions were **~24–34% higher** on the Monday immediately after spring-forward compared with the Monday before (93 vs. 125 heart attacks across four years in the underlying counts), and correspondingly lower after fall-back. The authors attribute this to the combination of acute sleep loss and the pre-existing "Monday effect" in cardiac events.

**Meta-analyses (2019–2020, e.g. in *Journal of Clinical Medicine*)** pooling multiple national datasets (Sweden, U.S./Michigan, Croatia, Germany and others) confirm a **higher AMI occurrence after the spring shift, with study-level increases ranging from about 4% to 29%**, generally larger in women and adults under 65. Not all findings are uniform, however: a large probabilistic U.S. nationwide study (published in a cardiology journal, 2023–2024) reported **no statistically robust difference in hospital deaths, strokes, or broader adverse cardiovascular event rates** tied to the transitions once probabilistic/statistical controls were applied — illustrating that, while the spring AMI-uptick finding is one of the most replicated in this literature, it is not undisputed at the level of aggregate mortality.

**Stroke:** Sipilä/Ruuskanen et al., using Finnish national hospitalization data (2004–2013, 14,834 ischemic stroke patients), found ischemic stroke hospitalizations rose with a relative risk (RR) of about **1.08 (8%)** in the first two days after a transition (either direction), with the increase most pronounced among **cancer patients (+25%)** and those **over age 65 (+20%)**. The whole-week effect was smaller and only borderline significant (RR ~1.03), suggesting the stroke risk bump is sharper but shorter-lived than the week-long crash/AMI effects.

**Atrial fibrillation:** A Montefiore Medical Center analysis of ~6,300 AF admissions (2009–2016) found AF hospital admissions rose from a yearly-average baseline of 2.56/day to **3.13/day (about +22%)** on the Monday–Thursday following spring-forward — an effect that held for women but not men, and that was **not observed after the fall transition**, again pointing to acute sleep loss (rather than daylight per se) as the proximate cause.

## 3. Other Health and Safety Effects

- **Workplace injuries (mining/general):** A Michigan State study (Barnes & Wagner, *Journal of Applied Psychology*, 2009) analyzing 576,292 U.S. mine injuries (1983–2006) found workers slept about **40 minutes less** the Sunday night of spring-forward (versus only ~12 minutes less after fall-back, a statistically insignificant change), and that on the Monday after spring-forward, workplace injuries rose **5.7%** and the resulting lost workdays rose by **~68% ("67.6%")** in severity/duration — i.e., not just more injuries but more serious ones.
- **Cyberloafing/attention:** Wagner et al. (Penn State, using six years of Google search data) found "cyberloafing" (non-work web browsing) was **3.1% higher** on the Monday after spring-forward than the Monday before, and **6.4% higher** than the following Monday; lab data showed each hour of lost sleep predicted about **8.4 more minutes of cyberloafing per task** (~20% of assigned task time).
- **Medical errors:** A Mayo Clinic-based analysis (published in *Journal of General Internal Medicine*) of voluntarily reported patient-safety incidents (2010–2017) found a statistically significant **18.7% increase** in incidents in the week after spring-forward (mostly medication-related errors — wrong dose/wrong drug); the fall-back increase (**~4.9%**) was not statistically significant. A related *Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine* analysis of medical malpractice claims found claims filed after the spring transition tended to involve **more severe patient harm**, consistent with an accumulated-circadian-misalignment mechanism rather than a purely acute one.
- **Suicide and mood:** An analysis of U.S. mortality data found the spring transition was associated with a **6.25% rise in the suicide rate** and a **6.59% rise** in combined suicide-and-substance-related deaths in the days immediately after spring-forward, with **no corresponding effect after fall-back** — again implicating acute sleep disruption specifically, not daylight exposure generally. Separately, clinicians and sleep researchers note DST transitions can exacerbate seasonal affective disorder (SAD) symptoms, anxiety, and depressive symptoms, though this literature is more mixed and observational than the suicide-mortality studies.
- **Sleep-deprivation mechanism:** Across nearly all of the above studies, researchers converge on the same causal story: the spring transition removes an hour of sleep opportunity practically overnight, and — unlike a single bad night — this loss compounds with circadian misalignment (the body's internal clock temporarily out of step with the new social/clock time), producing measurable upticks in accidents, cardiac events, and errors that peak in the first 2–4 days and mostly resolve within a week to ten days. The fall transition, which nominally *grants* an extra hour of sleep, shows far weaker or absent effects on these same sleep-linked outcomes — but introduces its own distinct hazard via abrupt evening darkness (see pedestrian-fatality findings above).

## 4. Economic Costs

- **"Sleepy Monday" / lost productivity:** Beyond the cyberloafing and workplace-injury findings above (Section 3), a peer-reviewed analysis of hourly GitHub commit activity (Dickinson & Waddell, *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*, forthcoming/2024) documents measurable productivity losses in the days following spring-forward, consistent with the broader "Sleepy Monday" narrative in the productivity literature.
- **Chmura Economics & Analytics "DST tax":** Chmura's widely cited "SleepBetter Lost-Hour Economic Index" estimates the annual U.S. economic cost of the spring clock change (combining lost productivity, increased heart attacks, workplace injuries in mining/construction, and other harms drawn from the peer-reviewed literature above) at **approximately $434 million per year** in earlier estimates, rising to **roughly $670–672 million per year** in Chmura's more recent (2024) update, which added categories such as stroke-related costs. These figures are industry/consultancy estimates built by aggregating the academic effect sizes above into dollar terms — useful as an order-of-magnitude signal, but they are not themselves peer-reviewed primary research and should be cited as such.
- **Energy savings — largely debunked or negligible:** The primary rationale historically offered for DST (energy conservation) has not held up under rigorous study. **Kotchen & Grant, "Does Daylight Saving Time Save Energy? Evidence from a Natural Experiment in Indiana"** (NBER Working Paper 14429; *Review of Economics and Statistics*, 2011) exploited Indiana's staggered county-level adoption of DST to analyze over seven million residential electricity meter-readings across three years. They found DST **increased**, rather than decreased, overall residential electricity demand by about **1%**, with the increase concentrated in **fall (2–4%)** — because reduced afternoon lighting demand was outweighed by increased morning heating and afternoon air-conditioning demand. They estimated the policy cost Indiana households about **$9 million/year** in extra electricity bills, plus **$1.7–5.5 million/year** in social costs from increased pollution. This remains the most rigorous empirical U.S. test of the energy-savings rationale for DST and is frequently cited as having substantially undercut it (older, cruder national estimates from the 1970s Department of Transportation had claimed ~1% savings, but those studies predate modern usage patterns — especially air conditioning penetration — and are considered outdated/unreliable by contemporary economists).

## 5. Critical Distinction: Costs of *Switching* vs. Costs of *Permanent DST*

Nearly all of the harms catalogued above (Sections 1–4) are costs of the **transition itself** — i.e., they would be eliminated by adopting *either* permanent standard time *or* permanent DST, since both remove the biannual clock-shock. This point is essential and is where much of the "end DST" advocacy and the more cautious medical-science position diverge sharply on *which* permanent state to choose.

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM), representing the U.S. sleep-medicine research and clinical community, has taken a clear and repeatedly reaffirmed position: **stop the clock changes, but land on permanent standard time, not permanent DST.** Its position statement — first issued 2020, updated and republished in the *Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine* in January 2024, and endorsed by 20 additional medical, scientific, and civic organizations — argues that standard time aligns much more closely with the sun and human circadian biology year-round, whereas permanent DST would leave the country in a state of chronic "social jet lag": clock time systematically ahead of solar time, delaying morning light exposure especially in winter, when — under permanent DST — sunrise would not occur until **after 8 a.m.** across much of the country and **after 9 a.m.** in parts of the northern and western time-zone edges. AASM links this chronic circadian misalignment to elevated long-run risk of obesity, metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular disease, and depression — a distinct and more diffuse harm from the acute, days-long spikes documented in Sections 1–4, but one the AASM considers more consequential over a full year.

**The precedent: the 1974 permanent-DST experiment.** The U.S. actually tried year-round DST once before, under a law signed by President Nixon in January 1974 amid the OPEC oil crisis. It was initially popular (79% approval in December 1973) but support collapsed within weeks once winter darkness set in — falling to 42% approval by February 1974 — as reports spread nationally of schoolchildren waiting at bus stops in pre-dawn darkness, carrying flashlights, or being escorted by parents for safety. In the weeks after the change, **eight children were killed in traffic accidents in Florida alone**, prompting Florida's governor to formally ask Congress to repeal the law. Congress obliged: both chambers passed a repeal bill in September 1974, and President Ford signed it on October 5, 1974, restoring standard time that October — after the experiment had lasted less than a year. This episode is the central historical exhibit sleep researchers and DST-policy skeptics cite when arguing that permanent DST (as opposed to permanent standard time) has already been tried, quickly produced dark-morning safety problems for children, and was abandoned once the political and public-safety costs became visible.

**Contemporary politics track this same fault line.** As of the House's July 14, 2026 passage of the Sunshine Protection Act (H.R. 139, vote 308–117), AASM formally condemned the move, with AASM president Dr. Fariha Abbasi-Feinberg stating the House "ignored the overwhelming evidence supporting the health and safety benefits of permanent standard time." AASM is instead backing a competing, bipartisan bill, the **Sunshine for Our Kids Act (H.R. 9638)**, introduced July 9, 2026 by Reps. Mary Gay Scanlon (D-PA) and Pat Harrigan (R-NC), which would make **standard time** permanent nationwide (with an opt-out for states that prefer DST) rather than DST. In the Senate, a companion Sunshine Protection Act effort stalled in the previous Congress after Sen. Tom Cotton (R-AR) raised the same dark-winter-morning concern that doomed the 1974 experiment — underscoring that the disagreement over H.R. 139 is not "keep the clock changes vs. end them" (virtually no one defends the switching itself) but specifically "permanent DST vs. permanent standard time."

## Bottom Line for Synthesis

- The transition-harm literature (spring crash/cardiac/injury/error/suicide spikes; fall pedestrian-darkness deaths) is genuinely one of the more replicated bodies of applied chronobiology research, spans multiple countries and multi-decade FARS/hospital datasets, and supports ending the biannual switch on public-health grounds with reasonably high confidence — though individual point estimates (e.g., 6% vs. 12% crash increase; 24% vs. 34% AMI increase) vary by dataset and methodology, and at least one large recent U.S. cardiovascular-outcomes study found no robust net mortality effect, so effect sizes should be presented as ranges, not single fixed numbers.
- Ending the switch is not the same question as which permanent time to choose. The dominant medical/sleep-science consensus (AASM, endorsed by 20+ organizations) holds that permanent standard time, not permanent DST, is the healthier landing point, based on circadian-alignment principles and reinforced by the empirical failure of the 1974 permanent-DST experiment.
- H.R. 139 as passed by the House on July 14, 2026 selects permanent DST, the option the sleep-medicine establishment opposes, setting up a direct clash with AASM and the competing Sunshine for Our Kids Act (H.R. 9638) as the bill moves to the Senate.
