# Locking the Clocks: The House Vote on the Sunshine Protection Act

*A synthesis of the health/economic research and a demographic analysis of Roll Call 238*

## Executive summary

On July 14, 2026, at 5:17 PM ET, the U.S. House of Representatives passed H.R. 139, the Sunshine Protection Act of 2025, by a vote of **308 to 117**, with 6 members not voting and none voting present. The bill would abolish the twice-yearly clock change and make daylight saving time (DST) — the "spring-forward" setting the country observes from March to November — the permanent, year-round standard nationwide, unless an individual state opts itself out before the act takes effect. Backed by President Trump, it now heads to a Senate where its prospects are genuinely uncertain: a companion effort stalled in the previous Congress, and opposition to the Senate version has come from both parties.

Two findings stand out. First, from the research: the large body of evidence marshaled in favor of ending the clock change is real and reasonably robust — but it argues against the *switching*, not for the specific destination the House chose. The medical consensus, led by the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM), holds that if the country stops changing clocks it should land on permanent **standard** time, not permanent DST — the exact option H.R. 139 selects, and the one the AASM formally condemned. Second, from the vote itself: this was a lopsidedly *Republican-driven* passage. Republicans backed it roughly nine-to-one (193–22), while Democrats split almost down the middle (114–95). The demographic story is more subtle than "old versus young," but the members who voted no were, on average, slightly older and notably more senior — a mean of 11.8 years in the House versus 9.6 years for the yes votes.

## What the research says

The scientific case against the biannual clock change rests on a convergent, multi-country, multi-decade literature. Crucially, nearly all of it concerns the *acute act of shifting the clock* — chiefly the March spring-forward and its one hour of lost sleep — rather than DST or standard time as steady states.

**Traffic fatalities.** The definitive study (Fritz et al., *Current Biology*, 2020; 732,835 fatal crashes in U.S. FARS data, 1996–2017) found fatal-crash risk rose about **6%** in the workweek after spring-forward, concentrated in morning crashes and in the western edges of time zones where dawn lags clock time — roughly **28 extra deaths a year**. The 2007 extension of DST into early March *doubled* the morning-crash effect, from ~5–6% to about **13%**, because March mornings are still dark. The same study found *no* significant fall-back effect and no evidence that DST's brighter evenings reduce crashes. A separate 2010–2019 FARS reanalysis put the spring occupant-fatality spike higher, near **12%** — the point estimates vary by dataset, but the direction is consistent. Fall's distinct hazard runs through a different mechanism: shifting sunset an hour earlier into the evening commute raises pedestrian and cyclist deaths about **13%**, with one estimate of ~**171 avoidable pedestrian deaths a year** if the autumn change were dropped.

**Cardiovascular effects.** Janszky & Ljung (*NEJM*, 2008; >100,000 Swedish heart attacks) found acute myocardial infarction (AMI) up about **5%** in the week after spring-forward and slightly *down* after fall-back. The "Michigan" study (Sandhu et al., *Open Heart*, 2014; 42,060 PCI admissions) found AMI admissions **~24–34% higher** on the Monday after spring-forward than the Monday before. Meta-analyses put the spring AMI increase across studies at **~4% to 29%**, larger in women and adults under 65. Related work found ischemic-stroke hospitalizations up ~**8%** in the two days after a transition (Finland; +25% in cancer patients, +20% in over-65s) and atrial-fibrillation admissions up ~**22%** after spring-forward (Montefiore). The important dissent: a large 2023/2024 probabilistic U.S. study found **no robust net effect** on aggregate hospital deaths or strokes — the field's most notable contrary result, and reason to treat the effect sizes as ranges rather than fixed figures.

**Other harms.** Spring-forward has been linked to a **5.7%** rise in Monday workplace injuries (and ~68% more severe lost-workday impact) in a 576,292-case mine-injury study; an **18.7%** jump in medical errors the following week (Mayo Clinic data, mostly medication errors); and a **6.25%** rise in the suicide rate — each with weak or absent fall-back counterparts, pointing to sleep loss rather than daylight as the mechanism.

**Economics.** A consultancy "DST tax" estimate (Chmura) aggregates these harms into roughly **$434 million**, updated to about **$670–672 million** a year — an order-of-magnitude signal, not peer-reviewed research. And the historical rationale for DST, energy savings, has been largely debunked: Kotchen & Grant (NBER WP 14429; 7M+ Indiana meter readings) found DST *increased* residential electricity use about **1%** overall (2–4% in fall) as heating and air-conditioning demand outweighed lighting savings, costing Indiana households ~$9 million a year.

**The crucial nuance — and the irony.** Almost every harm above is a cost of *switching*, so it would be eliminated by permanent standard time or permanent DST equally. The disagreement is over which destination to pick. The AASM's position statement (*Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine*, Jan. 2024, endorsed by 20+ organizations) argues for permanent **standard** time: it aligns with solar time and circadian biology year-round, whereas permanent DST imposes chronic "social jet lag," with winter sunrise after **8 a.m.** across much of the country and after **9 a.m.** at northern and western time-zone edges — linked to long-run obesity, metabolic, cardiovascular, and depression risk. The precedent looms large: the U.S. tried permanent DST in 1974, approval collapsed from **79%** to **42%** within weeks as children waited for buses in pre-dawn darkness (eight were killed in Florida traffic accidents), and Congress repealed it inside a year. The irony is direct — the very harms bill supporters cite come from the switching, which either landing point would fix, yet H.R. 139 selects the destination the sleep-medicine establishment says is the wrong one. The AASM "condemned" the House vote and backs a rival bill, the Sunshine for Our Kids Act (H.R. 9638), for permanent standard time.

## The vote by the numbers

**Totals.** Passed 308–117; 0 present; 6 not voting; 431 members cast a Yea or Nay.

**Party splits.** Republicans 193–22 (roughly 9-to-1 for). Democrats 114–95 — a near-even split that supplied all but a handful of the no votes. The chamber's single Independent voted Yea.

**Age.** Age was, at most, a faint signal. The mean age of Yea voters was **58.6** (median 58.0), barely younger than Nay voters at **59.5** (median 59.8). Yea ages ran from **32.4 to 88.5**; Nay from **29.5 to 87.1**. The age-decade gradient is non-monotonic — support was actually *highest* in the 40s (81% Yea) and 80s (78%), and lowest among the handful of members in their 30s (62%) and the single member in his 20s (0%). There is no clean "young members hate the clock change" story in the data.

**Tenure.** The sharper contrast is seniority. Nay voters averaged **11.8 years** in the House versus **9.6 years** for Yea voters — the members who voted no were, on balance, longer-serving. (Medians are close, 7.7 vs 7.5 years, so the gap is driven by a heavier tail of very senior no votes.)

**Superlatives.** Youngest Yea: Brandon Gill (R-TX), 32.4. Oldest Yea: Harold "Hal" Rogers (R-KY), 88.5. Youngest Nay: Maxwell Frost (D-FL), 29.5. Oldest Nay: Steny Hoyer (D-MD), 87.1.

**The six not voting** (mean age 58.1): Maxine Dexter (D-OR, 53.6); Cleo Fields (D-LA, 63.6); John James (R-MI, 45.1 — youngest); Teresa Leger Fernández (D-NM, 67.0); Nancy Mace (R-SC, 48.6); and Mariannette Miller-Meeks (R-IA, 70.9 — oldest). Three Democrats, three Republicans.

## Interesting tidbits

- **The lone Independent decided nothing but is a nice footnote.** Kevin Kiley — listed as an Independent from California, age 41.5 — cast the House's only Independent vote, a Yea — recorded directly on the official Clerk tally page, where his name appears underlined (the Clerk's Independent marker) in the Yea list.
- **A soundtrack for the tally.** Rep. Scott DesJarlais (R-TN), presiding, played the Beatles' "Here Comes the Sun" from his phone as he read out the result.
- **The 20s cast one vote, and it was no.** The chamber's single member in his twenties, Maxwell Frost (D-FL), voted Nay — making the 20–29 band 0% Yea on a sample of one.
- **A real gender gap — mostly among Democrats.** *(My own cut, computed from the CSVs.)* Women split 70–50 (58% Yea); men 238–67 (78% Yea). But this is largely party composition: within the GOP, both sexes backed the bill ~90% (women 93%, men 89%), while among Democrats women were markedly cooler than men — **48% Yea (44–48) versus 60% (70–47)**. The overall women-versus-men gap is real but concentrated on the Democratic side.
- **Freshmen were the keenest.** *(My own cut.)* First-term members voted 55–12, **82% Yea**, versus 71% for everyone else — consistent with the seniority pattern, since the longest-serving members supplied the heaviest no vote.
- **The historical southern-opposition prediction did *not* show up.** *(My own cut.)* Low-latitude/southern states have historically resisted extending DST because it darkens winter mornings. In the raw numbers the Census South actually looks *more* favorable (80% Yea vs 68% elsewhere) — but that is entirely a party artifact, because the South sends more Republicans. Controlling for party, the regional gap vanishes: Southern Democrats 53% Yea vs non-Southern Democrats 55%; Southern Republicans 92% vs non-Southern 88%. Whatever drove the historical latitude split, it left no trace in this roll call once party is held constant.

## Caveats and provenance

- **Sources.** Vote facts and party breakdown come from `raw/votes/vote_metadata.md` (verified against the House Clerk record and multiple news outlets). Age, tenure, and position statistics come from `synthesis/vote_age_stats.md`; the per-member votes and demographics I re-cut come from `raw/votes/roll238_votes.csv` and `raw/members/house_members.csv`, with all 431 voting members joined to demographics by Bioguide ID (my join reproduced the official 308 / 117 / 6 tally exactly). All research claims trace to `research/transition-costs.md`.
- **Age and tenure definitions.** Ages are as of the vote date (July 14, 2026). Tenure is measured from each member's first House term start and can *overstate* continuous service for members with interrupted careers, which likely inflates the "very senior" tail somewhat. My cuts labeled "freshman" use `total_terms == 1`.
- **Correlation, not causation.** The age and tenure differences are small and heavily confounded with party; nothing here establishes that age or seniority *caused* any member's vote. The age-decade gradient is non-monotonic and should not be read as a trend.
- **Small-*n* caution.** The six not-voting members and the single-Independent cell are tiny samples; the 20–29 age band contains exactly one member. Treat every one of these as color, not as a rate. Regional and gender cuts are descriptive splits of a single roll call, not modeled effects, and my region grouping uses the Census definition of "the South" as a rough latitude proxy.
